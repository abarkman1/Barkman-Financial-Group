Medicare Questions

Medicare is genuinely confusing. Here's what most people need to know.

Medicare Supplement (Medigap) works alongside Original Medicare. Your supplement plan pays the costs Medicare doesn't cover — deductibles, co-pays, and coinsurance. You can see any doctor in the country who accepts Medicare, with no network restrictions.

Medicare Advantage (Part C) replaces your Original Medicare. You work within the plan's network of doctors, and the plan manages your coverage. Advantage plans often have lower monthly premiums but can have higher out-of-pocket costs when you actually need care.

Which is right for you depends on your health, your doctors, and how you use healthcare. We'll walk you through both options clearly.

You're eligible for Medicare at 65. Your Initial Enrollment Period is a 7-month window — 3 months before your birthday month, your birthday month, and 3 months after. Enrolling late can result in lifetime premium penalties, so timing matters significantly.

If you're still working and covered by employer insurance at 65, the rules are different. Call us before you turn 65 and we'll make sure you don't miss anything important.

Medicare Advantage plans can be changed during Annual Enrollment (October 15 – December 7 each year). For Medicare Supplement plans, the rules vary by state — in most states, you can only switch without medical underwriting during specific windows. This is exactly why we do annual reviews — to make sure you're in the right plan before any window closes.

Final Expense

Coverage designed to protect your family when you're gone.

Final expense is a type of whole life insurance — it never expires and builds cash value over time. It's designed for smaller coverage amounts ($5,000–$35,000) specifically intended to cover funeral costs, medical bills, and end-of-life expenses. Supplemental Coverage

Filling the gaps before they cost you.

Even good health insurance has deductibles, co-pays, and out-of-pocket maximums that add up quickly during a serious illness. Supplemental coverage pays cash directly to you — not the hospital — which you can use for anything: your deductible, lost wages, rent, groceries, or whatever you need. A critical illness plan pays a lump sum if you're diagnosed with cancer, heart attack, or stroke — exactly when you need financial flexibility most.
