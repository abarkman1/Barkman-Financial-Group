Understanding Medicare

Medicare has four parts.
Each covers something different.

Original Medicare from the government has gaps — it does not cover everything, and what it doesn't cover can be significant. Understanding what each part covers helps you understand what private insurance fills in.

A
Medicare Part A

Hospital Insurance

Covers inpatient hospital stays, skilled nursing facility care, hospice care, and some home health services. Most people get Part A premium-free if they or their spouse worked and paid Medicare taxes for at least 10 years.

Most people: $0 premium · Hospital deductible applies
B
Medicare Part B

Medical Insurance

Covers doctor visits, outpatient care, preventive services, and medical equipment. Part B has a monthly premium, an annual deductible, and typically covers 80% of approved costs — leaving you responsible for 20% with no out-of-pocket limit.

Standard premium ~$174/mo (2024) · 20% coinsurance after deductible
C
Medicare Part C

Medicare Advantage

A private insurance alternative to Original Medicare. Part C plans bundle Part A, Part B, and usually Part D together. Often includes dental, vision, and hearing. Network-based. Many plans have $0 monthly premium. Administered by private carriers.

Often $0 premium · Network restrictions apply · Out-of-pocket maximum
D
Medicare Part D

Prescription Drug Coverage

Standalone prescription drug coverage added to Original Medicare or a Supplement plan. Each plan has its own formulary — the list of drugs it covers and at what cost. Formularies change every year. We check your exact medications against every plan before recommending one.

Monthly premium varies · Late enrollment penalty if missed
The gap Original Medicare leaves — and why it matters

Original Medicare (Parts A and B) covers roughly 80% of approved medical costs. The remaining 20% has no cap. A major surgery, extended hospital stay, or serious illness can leave you with tens of thousands of dollars in out-of-pocket costs. This is the gap that Medicare Supplement (Medigap) plans are designed to fill — and why choosing the right coverage structure matters more than most people realize.

Your Coverage Options

Three paths through Medicare.
One right answer for your situation.

We don't push one type over another. The right path depends on your doctors, your medications, your budget, and how often you use healthcare.

Most Flexible
Medicare Supplement (Medigap)
Works alongside Original Medicare to cover the gaps

A Medigap policy is private insurance that pays for costs Original Medicare doesn't cover — deductibles, copayments, and coinsurance. Plans are standardized by letter (Plan G, Plan N, etc.) so benefits are identical across carriers. The only difference between carriers is the premium. You keep your Medicare card, see any doctor who accepts Medicare, and get coverage nationwide.

  • See any doctor or specialist who accepts Medicare — no network
  • Coverage applies anywhere in the country
  • Predictable, capped out-of-pocket costs
  • No referrals required
  • Guaranteed renewable — cannot be cancelled due to health changes
  • Higher monthly premium than Advantage plans
  • Does not include drug coverage — need separate Part D
  • Medical underwriting may apply after Initial Enrollment Period
Most Popular
Medicare Advantage (Part C)
All-in-one coverage through a private carrier

Medicare Advantage plans are an alternative to Original Medicare. You still have Medicare, but a private insurer manages your coverage. Most plans bundle Part A, Part B, and Part D together and often add dental, vision, and hearing benefits that Original Medicare doesn't cover. Most plans have a $0 or very low monthly premium. You use a network of doctors and hospitals — coverage outside the network may be limited or unavailable.

  • Often $0 monthly premium
  • Bundled medical, prescription drug, dental, vision, and hearing
  • Out-of-pocket maximum protects against catastrophic costs
  • Coordinated care within a provider network
  • Network restrictions — not all doctors are covered
  • Prior authorization may be required for some services
  • Plans vary significantly by county and carrier
Prescription Only
Medicare Part D
Standalone prescription drug coverage

If you have Original Medicare plus a Supplement, you need a standalone Part D plan for prescription drug coverage. Each plan maintains its own formulary — the list of drugs it covers, which tier they're on, and what you'll pay at the pharmacy. Formularies change every year, which is why we review every client's Part D coverage annually during Annual Enrollment Period.

  • We match your exact medication list to each plan's formulary
  • We compare total annual drug costs — not just monthly premiums
  • Coverage reviewed annually — formularies change every year
  • Late enrollment penalty if you go without coverage too long
  • Coverage details vary significantly between plans
  • Must be reviewed annually — last year's best plan may not be best now

Enrollment Periods

When you can enroll —
and why timing is critical

Missing an enrollment window can result in permanent late enrollment penalties and restricted access to Supplement plans. Call us before you need to enroll — not after.

Initial Enrollment Period

Turning 65

Opens 3 months before your 65th birthday month · Closes 3 months after

This is the most protected enrollment window you'll ever have. During IEP, you can enroll in any Medicare plan without medical underwriting — meaning pre-existing conditions cannot affect your eligibility or premium for Supplement plans. This window does not come back.

If you miss this window, Medigap carriers can use medical underwriting in most states — meaning you could be charged more or denied coverage based on your health history.
Special Enrollment Period

Leaving Employer Coverage

Triggered by qualifying life events

If you or your spouse had employer-sponsored coverage past 65, you qualify for a Special Enrollment Period when that coverage ends. You have 8 months to enroll in Part B without a late enrollment penalty. Call us as soon as you know your end date — the window is shorter than most people realize.

Do not rely on COBRA or a spouse's retiree coverage as a substitute for Medicare enrollment — this can still trigger late enrollment penalties.
Annual Enrollment Period

October 15 – December 7

Every year — changes effective January 1

During AEP, anyone on Medicare can switch Advantage plans, switch from Advantage back to Original Medicare, or change Part D plans. This is why we review every client annually. Formularies change, network restrictions change, and premiums change. The best plan last year may not be the best plan this year.

Open Enrollment Period

January 1 – March 31

For Medicare Advantage enrollees only

If you enrolled in a Medicare Advantage plan during AEP and want to switch plans or return to Original Medicare, you can do so during OEP. This window is for corrections — it's not an alternative to making a careful choice during AEP. Changes made here take effect the first of the following month.
